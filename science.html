Science

Ferroptosis, by subtraction

Most brain tumor drugs must cross the blood–brain barrier to work. Cyst(e)inase does not. It depletes cysteine and cystine throughout the circulation, lowering the supply available to glioblastoma cells. The enzyme stays in the blood; its effect reaches the tumor.

The dependency

Why glioblastoma needs cyst(e)ine

Cancer cells import cystine, the oxidized form of cysteine, largely through the system xc− transporter. Inside the cell it becomes cysteine, the limiting building block of glutathione.

Glutathione lets the enzyme GPX4 clear lipid peroxides from cell membranes. When cysteine runs short, glutathione falls, lipid peroxides accumulate and the cell dies by ferroptosis.

The enzyme

What Cyst(e)inase does

Cyst(e)inase is an engineered human enzyme that degrades both cysteine and cystine in the bloodstream, depleting the pool that tumors draw from, with no need to cross the BBB.

L-cysteine → Cyst(e)inase → pyruvate + NH3 + H2S

Because it is derived from a human protein, it was designed with tolerability in mind.

The differentiator

Working around the blood–brain barrier

The blood–brain barrier blocks most drugs from reaching the brain. This is a major reason brain tumors are difficult to treat.

Cyst(e)inase works by systemic substrate depletion. It lowers cysteine and cystine levels in the blood, which reduces the supply available to the tumor. The enzyme itself does not need to enter the brain.

Combination

Pairing with temozolomide

Temozolomide is the standard chemotherapy for newly diagnosed glioblastoma. In published preclinical studies, Cyst(e)inase synergizes with and reverses resistance to temozolomide, pointing to a path that directly adds to standard care therapy, rather than replacing it.
